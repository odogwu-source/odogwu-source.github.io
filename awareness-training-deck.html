Cybersecurity Awareness & Social Engineering Defence

A practical employee-facing awareness session designed for a simulated organisation. The material focuses on behaviour employees can apply immediately: recognise manipulation, verify suspicious requests, protect credentials and report incidents quickly.

01

Why People Are Targeted

Attackers often exploit trust, routine and emotion instead of attacking technology directly. Awareness reduces the chance that a convincing message becomes an organisational incident.

  • Pause before acting on unusual requests
  • Treat urgency as a reason to verify, not rush
  • Security is a shared organisational responsibility
02

Recognise Social Engineering

Common techniques include phishing, spear phishing, business email compromise, smishing, vishing, impersonation, pretexting and baiting.

  • Authority: “The CEO needs this now”
  • Urgency: “Your account expires today”
  • Fear: “You have violated company policy”
  • Curiosity/reward: unexpected files, prizes or opportunities
03

Spot a Suspicious Message

  • Unexpected sender or look-alike domain
  • Unusual payment, password or MFA request
  • Generic greeting or inconsistent tone
  • Link destination differs from displayed text
  • Unexpected attachment or cloud-sharing prompt
  • Pressure to bypass normal procedure
04

Verify Before You Trust

Do not verify a suspicious request using the contact details inside the suspicious message itself.

  • Use a known phone number or trusted directory
  • Confirm payment/account changes out-of-band
  • Navigate to known sites directly instead of clicking unsolicited links
  • Escalate unusual executive or vendor requests
05

Protect Accounts & Credentials

  • Use unique, strong passwords or an approved password manager
  • Enable MFA wherever available
  • Never disclose passwords or authentication codes
  • Question unexpected MFA prompts
  • Report suspected credential exposure immediately
06

Safe Email, Links & Attachments

  • Inspect sender addresses carefully
  • Hover/check links before opening where supported
  • Be cautious with unexpected documents and archives
  • Do not enable macros or security-bypassing features on request
  • When uncertain, report rather than experiment
07

Smishing & Vishing

Social engineering is not limited to email. SMS, messaging apps and phone calls can create the same pressure.

  • Do not disclose credentials or codes over unsolicited calls
  • Verify callers through an independently obtained contact method
  • Be suspicious of QR codes and shortened links in unexpected messages
08

Business Email Compromise

Payment and supplier-change requests deserve extra verification because attackers may impersonate executives, vendors or colleagues.

  • Follow established payment approval procedures
  • Verify changes to bank details independently
  • Watch for secrecy, urgency and process-bypass requests
09

If You Clicked — Act Quickly

Fast reporting can reduce impact. Employees should not hide mistakes or wait to see what happens.

  • Stop further interaction with the suspicious content
  • Report through the organisation's approved security channel
  • Explain what happened accurately
  • Follow IT/security instructions for credential reset or device isolation
10

Report the Attempt

Reporting suspicious communications helps defenders investigate whether the same campaign is targeting other employees and enables blocking of malicious indicators.

  • Use the approved phishing-report mechanism
  • Do not forward malicious content casually to colleagues
  • Include useful context when reporting
11

Employee Decision Model

STOP → INSPECT → VERIFY → REPORT

  • STOP before reacting to urgency
  • INSPECT sender, request, link and context
  • VERIFY through a trusted independent channel
  • REPORT suspicious activity promptly
12

Knowledge Check

  • Would you approve an urgent bank-detail change from email alone?
  • What should you do with an unexpected MFA prompt?
  • How should you verify a suspicious executive request?
  • Who should receive a suspected phishing report?
  • What four steps make up the decision model?

FACILITATOR OUTCOME

What employees should be able to do after this session

Recognise common social-engineering cues, challenge unusual requests, use trusted verification channels, protect authentication information, and report suspected incidents without delay.

DESIGN BASIS

Why this programme is structured this way

NIST SP 800-50 Rev. 1 treats cybersecurity learning as a lifecycle programme intended to encourage behaviour change and strengthen security culture. NIST SP 800-171 Rev. 3 explicitly includes recognising and reporting social engineering in security literacy training. CISA guidance likewise emphasises teaching employees to identify phishing indicators, report suspicious correspondence and use strong authentication. This artefact applies those principles to a concise employee-facing session.
